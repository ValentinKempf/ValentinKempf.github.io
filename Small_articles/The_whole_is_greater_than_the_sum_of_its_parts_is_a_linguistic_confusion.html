The whole is greater than the sum of its parts is just a linguistic confusion

There is a common saying: „the whole is greater than the sum of its parts“. This saying is nothing more than a confusion about what the word „part“ means; depending on how you define „part“, it is either trivially true or trivially false.

Sometimes we talk about parts as material parts, for instance a table might have as parts: wood and nails. A human has as parts atomic parts: hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, phosphorous etc. The whole is certainly greater than the sum of material parts, a pile of wood and nails is not a table, a sack of water with a bunch of carbon and phosphorous suspended in it is not a man. Nobody doubts this and this is trivially true.

Sometimes we talk about parts as formal parts. Let's say someone takes a 3cm cross-section of my thigh and redistributes the muscle and bone of that area to other parts of my bodies, such that I'd be 3cm shorter than I am right now. This operation did not change my material parts, but obviously it did change the properties of the whole, because I'm now shorter than before the operation. But it also changed some of my parts, namely my formal parts. It is perfectly reasonable to say: it is part of me, that I'm 178cm tall. People talk like this all the time and they are not using metaphors when they do. Someone might say: „being stubborn is part of who I am“; they are not picturing that there is some material thing in their brain called „stubbornness“ that can be cut out and inspected using a magnifying glass or painted by an artist. To make it understandable to the philosopher, they are saying: „my mind and body are arranged in such a way that I react stubbornly“. [1]

Some people might protest and say: the reason why the whole is greater than the sum of its parts, is because there is some special soul or purpose that isn't captured by the parts and their relations. But this new soul is either an immaterial thing (i.e. a material part) or it is a manner in which already known parts are arranged (i.e. a formal part). If somebody proposes his soul or purpose or whatever, is neither a material nor a formal part of him, he is strictly speaking non-sense and using words with no meaning. And then souls and purposes can't be said to be part of anything, let alone himself.

So if we accept formal parts as proper parts (as common sense does), then „the whole is greater than the sum of its part“ is completely and utterly wrong. As wrong as saying that a tree has regret; it is something, which is prohibited by common-sense use of language. Of course such a use of language is not universally prohibited by grammar, since there is a certain corner of mysticism, in which such a claim is acceptable; it is also accepted by some, who think of themselves as philosophers, but they are just bad philosophers.

There is perhaps no better example for the thesis espoused by Wittgenstein that the problems of philosophy result from a confusion of language.

[1] When philosophers analyse a certain phrase p , they are often tempted to say: „what p is really saying is q “. What the philosopher is in fact doing is not uncovering some hidden deeper meaning (what is really meant), they are simply translating from one type of language game (everyday language) to another (formal philosophical language). Philosophical language might be more clear or more precise in certain circumstances, but it is not „what is really meant“. The real intended meaning behind what is said, is exactly what is being said. In fact you can use this method the other way around: Non-philosopher: what is all this possible worlds stuff about? What is this weird phrase doing: „there is a possible world, in which X is the case“. Me: what they really mean is: „ X is possible“ This dialogue is just as realistic and shows just as proper a use of language as what the philosopher is tempted to use.
