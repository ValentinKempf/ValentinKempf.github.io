Libertarianism and the schizophrenic view of reasons

This is a sort of companion piece to my other article on why libertarianism is wrong. It touches on a similar theme, but didn't have a nice place in there. Feel free to enjoy it on its own, since I won't assume any prior knowledge.

So what libertarians would like to have is (1) that their choices are not determined by prior events and (2) that their decisions can be rationally explained.

Retrospective reasons and non-rational choice

Nathan Hawkins illustrates the results from these two aspects in very lucid terms [1]. He considers the example of a decision he has to make about where to go on holiday. He can either decide to go to Spain or to Italy, and he has reasons for both. He might decide to go to Spain because of the beaches and architecture, or he might decide to go to Italy because of the wine and historic sites.

In the moment he makes the decision, he chooses to act upon certain reasons and the reasons he chose retroactively become [2] the reasons for that action. Let's say in our universe he decides to go to Italy, then he has gone there because of the wine and historic sites. But there is also a possible universe, which is identical in every way up to the point of him making the decision, but where he decides to go to Spain. In this parallel universe, his decision was motivated by the beaches and architecture. In both cases, he can give a rational explanation for the decision he made.

It's important to point out that in this model each choice has a rational and a non-rational part to it. The rational part is the reasons he can give for his decision. But there is also a non-rational part of the decision, which determines which reasons are the reasons that matter. He chooses which of the reasons determine his decision. This choice over what reasons matter is itself something that happens for no reason; there's no reason why, in our universe, Nathan finds the wine and historic sites more compelling and there's equally no reason why in the parallel universe he finds the beaches and architecture more compelling [3].

The forcing account of reasons

However, there is another account of reasons, where a reason really constrains what you can rationally choose to do or believe. Imagine somebody were to give to you a proof that the Pythagorean theorem is true and you understood all parts of it. Then there's no rational room open to not believing in the Pythagorean theorem. A reason compels you to believe or it is no reason at all.

Furthermore, this is not really a restriction of my freedom, since you can't meaningfully want to be logically wrong. In fact, I personally believe, I have the tremendous freedom to always follow the argument that is the most convincing to me. And I see no benefit in having the possibility to choose irrational beliefs.

Certainly nobody would be proud of using their free will to take an indeterministic stance in a debate. Imagine someone says: it is completely true that in some parallel universe I agree with your argument against libertarianism, but it is a brute fact with no prior causes that I happen to disagree with you on this point. You would certainly doubt the ability of that person to engage in effective logical argumentation at all. Or if they say it's a non-rational choice I made to disagree with you, then you would accuse that person of being completely arbitrary. Again: a reason forces you to believe in something in all possible worlds, or it is no reason at all.

The aesthetics of reason vs the logic of reason

Now I will admit that the account proposed by Nathan Hawkins sounds compelling and intuitive, but the forcing account of reasons sounds compelling and intuitive as well. So we are left at a stalemate. I don't have a method for resolving the stalemate, but I can give an account of how it came about.

It seems that both our accounts are more compelling for our own example than for the example of the other. On the one hand, when deciding which logical, scientific or philosophical argument is sound, it doesn't seem like there is a non-rational choice, because the reasons are forcing. On the other hand, when you are deciding on your next holiday destination or which flavour of ice-cream to buy, it doesn't seem like reasons have to uniquely determine your decision, because there is a non-rational aspect to the choice.

Maybe then, both accounts are valid each in their respective domain. The forcing account of reasons is true for all logical, scientific, and philosophical reasons, while the non-rational choice account of reasons is true for art, food, and other areas of subjective taste.

On the other hand, we should try to have a single unified account of reasoning, because we don't want to split reasoning up arbitrarily into different kinds. It doesn't seem like reasoning in science or mathematics is based on a totally different system than reasoning in the arts. So we have to make the choice of what account we want to apply to the domain of the other [4]. Either we start with the aesthetics of reason and apply them to the sciences and logic or we start with the logic of reason and apply it to taste and the arts. And I am a big proponent of the logic of reason.

Now, the one problem you could pose to the aesthetics of reason is that it is not aesthetically pleasing. This kind of account of reason seems to introduce a dangerous split into our psyche, with one part choosing for no reason and the other one giving reasons for the choice. This is a kind of schizophrenic view of reasoning, where it is not used by the A part of the brain that actually makes the decision, but only by the B part of the brain that justifies decisions to others. I hope the following paragraphs will make it clearer what I mean by that.

Libertarianism as a form of choice blindness

ü In psychology, there is this concept of choice blindness. Basically, people are very good at rationalizing choices they never made. In a famous study, test subjects had to choose which of two faces they found more attractive, and then later they were handed back the images and asked to give reasons why they made those choices. But unbeknownst to them, their choices had been changed using sleight of hand, and they were actually justifying a choice they never made [5]. Importantly, we all understand that these reasons are complete bullshit; we make them up after the fact to save face.

What does this have to do with the libertarian? Recall Nathan’s decision between going to Spain or to Italy. Suppose we rerun the moment of decision and, in one world, he picks Italy and in the other Spain. In each world, we can ask him to explain his choice, and he’ll give you a sincere and reasonable answer. Nathan stands ready to affirm it as his own, no matter what happens. Everything in the world and everything about himself can be the same, yet in one universe he finds the wine and historic sights more convincing and in another he finds the beaches and architecture more convincing. He can’t tell you in advance which answer he would give, because he doesn’t know which choice will happen to come into his mind, but whichever one he makes, he’ll experience it as expressing his true nature, and he’ll retrospectively find reasons that seem to explain it perfectly. Even though these reasons have nothing to do with the choice and arguably only little to do with himself.

You might respond to this example: „well, who cares if Nathan retroactively constructs a justification for his choice of where to go on holiday. If you think this way of going about things is irrational, then let him be irrational. Your opinions don’t matter here“. That is a fair response for the above example, but consider this example. Picture a judge who is giving out a sentence to the defendant with free will. Let’s say she could give the defendant three years or five. Whichever number occurs to her, she writes down reasons explaining why she think that sentence was justified, citing the gravity of the offense, precedent, mitigating factors and so on. But what if that number itself was not settled by any of these reasons. It just unpredictably came about in her head, and the justification was assembled afterward to rationalize whichever number came out. We’d say she picked a number and dressed it up. The written opinion, however eloquent, would be worthless as a justification, because it had no influence on the choice being made; it was fitted to the choice post hoc. All our outrage would be justified: a person’s years in prison were decided by nothing, and backed up with a story only after the facts. There will be years of suffering downstream of an arbitrary happenstance covered up by a deceiving pseudo-justification.
