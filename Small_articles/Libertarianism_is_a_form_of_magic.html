Libertarianism is a form of magic

One of the most common conceptions about free will involves two aspects:

  1. That your freely willed decisions are not determined by prior causes. That is: in any given situation, where you have free will, you could have chosen otherwise
  2. That your free decision is actually made by yourself

For the sake of brevity, I will call this position libertarianism, although some libertarians might not agree, that this accurately portrays their position.

I will show that if these two aspects are compatible the reasons for their compatibility are completely unknowable in principle. And thus the only genuine way to save this kind of free will is to directly invoke magic.

The central thought experiment

Suppose you are in a situation, where you are about to make a free choice. Let's say for simplicity, that you are choosing between two courses of action A and B. We use this simple example purely for convenience, so that we don't have to constantly talk about myriads of different possible decisions, but just two. Once you grasp the case of two courses of action, it is trivial to generalize it to an arbitrary number of different courses of action.

Also suppose that a few moments before you make your choice, there is a quasi-omniscient observer, who knows everything that can be known about the universe in that very moment. Using the laws of physics he could calculate the probability of you making choices A and B. This will always be possible whether the laws of physics are ultimately deterministic or not. If they are deterministic, then one choice will have probability 1 and the other will have 0; if the laws of physics are not ultimately deterministic, then one of them will have probability p between 0 and 1 and the other will have probability 1-p.

Since determinism obviously precludes the existence of this kind of free will, we will grant for the sake of argument that one of the events happens with probability p and the other with probability 1-p. And we will note that whatever determines our choosing A or B, must in principle be unknowable, since our quasi-omniscient observer couldn't figure it out with complete certainty, despite having access to all knowable states of affairs.[1]

In this thought experiment aspect 1) is already satisfied. Now it's necessary to find a way to incorporate aspect 2) into this picture, that your choices are determined by you or some part of you. But above we have already concluded that whatever determines your action must stay mysterious and unknowable in principle.

This, to put it bluntly, is a direct invocation of magic. But magical thinking shouldn't be rejected outright, so we will see whether there can be rational justification for this stance.

Can you justify this position?

Our libertarian has to justify his belief in some missing ingredient determining the choice. The missing ingredient could be sheer nothingness itself. But you are not identical to nothingness — so if the choice comes from nothingness, it doesn't come from you. And if it doesn't come from you, it's not your free will. Therefore, to defend free will, we have to find a way of justifying the existence of a thing that acts as the missing ingredient.

Now, it's very hard to know that something exists, without being able to pin down at least some of its properties. For instance, you might verify the existence of crows by observing a crow; but you can't observe a crow without also observing its properties, like its approximate size, colour, shape etc. In another instance you might prove that the equation x 2 − 2 = 0 has a real solution, but you can't prove that fact without also knowing some properties of the solution, that it is a real number, that it is less than infinity etc.

Even in cases where we don't know a lot, we still know something about the thing whose existence we believe in. We don't know a whole lot about dark matter, but we can make some statements about its distribution and how much of it there is (there is more dark matter than regular matter). Even in the case where your house has been robbed, you can still infer that while you were gone some agent (realistically a human) has come in and stolen your belongings, because they wanted them. And therefore there is no example where you know of something's existence, without being able to pin down any of its properties.

But this existence without knowability is exactly what the proponent of free will needs to argue for. They need to find some evidence that justifies them believing in this missing ingredient without giving the quasi-omniscient observer any ability to predict what they are going to do. They have to prove the existence of an unpredictable determiner. At the very least this is going to be very difficult; I would propose that it is impossible to find such a thing. It's not that they lack evidence for their position. It's that evidence for their position doesn't even seem attainable or coherent.

Whether this argument succeeds or not, it also just seems implausible that there is some part of „you“ that is an unknowable decision maker. I am a person in the world with a certain weight, height, and I have certain beliefs and preferences. All these things are knowable in principle and even in practice to some extent. These are the kind of things that explain my decisions, and other people are, at least in principle, able to access them. We are all able to predict the decisions of others to some degree, so it seems like knowable properties of somebody are exactly the kind of thing that sways you toward certain choices and away from others, not some unknowable mystery.

Another implausible consequence is that you yourself wouldn't be able to know the decision making part in you. Even if you are okay being skeptical about the mental life of others, you presumably still want to know how your own decisions came about. Yet the libertarian has to bar these facts behind the veil of unknowability.

To recap: the libertarian has to justify the existence of some thing, whose determining properties are in principle unknowable, but that seems incoherent, since justifications for existence of a thing always have to deal with its properties. And even if such a justification is coherent, you would be placed in an absurdly skeptical position about the mental life of others and even of yourself, which most people don't tolerate.

How to ground morality

Libertarians often state that indeterminism is necessary for moral responsibility. But we also agree that if it is just completely arbitrary randomness, then it does not result in moral responsibility; so the libertarian wants to somehow argue that they can have indeterminism without it devolving into uncontrollable randomness.

But in order to make this distinction, they have to explain what makes that difference and they have to justify that our normal decision making exemplifies these features that delineate it from arbitrary randomness. Meanwhile they are also committed to the stance that his decision is determined by an unpredictable determiner. Now we can state the challenge to the libertarian concretely: can you peer into an unpredictable determiner and say: „that's what distinguishes it from randomness“? Even if we grant that such an explanation of what makes a choice non-random is intelligible, we still have to be convinced that this explanation is exemplified in what we actually do.

This argument is related to, but not quite the same as those of the previous section. Above we discussed how we can justify the existence of an unpredictable determiner. Now we have to justify what makes this unpredictable determiner different from sheer randomness. So in reality the libertarian has to answer three very difficult questions: a) how do you know that this unpredictable determiner exists b) how do you explain what makes this unpredictable determiner different from mere chance c) how do you know that the individual unpredictable determiner in yourself demonstrates this quality that makes it non-random?

Who is pessimistic here?

Libertarians often talk about their view as the warm cuddly one, the one you would like to be real, even if it is not. In the same breath they suppose that everybody else lives in a cold and harsh world of mechanistic brutality, alienation and loneliness in an arbitrary uncaring universe imposing its will on you by inexorable causal chains.

First of all, we need not think of the laws of nature as something forced upon matter externally. No part of science tells you that the laws of nature are like some evil dictator that brutally tortures every particle that steps out of line, until it is totally obedient to every whim of this cosmic demon. This pessimistic judgement on the world is not a scientific theory, it is just as unsupported by science as the view that everything autonomously serves the unfolding of a higher purpose. Science and determinism don't rule out this pessimistic view of determinism — that's correct — but neither do they rule out the optimistic one. The libertarians just assert that the determinists must hold this pessimistic view of reality, because either they are unable to imagine others having a different view of the world or they dishonestly misrepresent the views of others to further their interests. In fact, there is nothing about determinism that presupposes a bad judgement over a good judgement. How you evaluate this factual view about the world is completely up to you.

There is also nothing stopping the compatibilist from turning the tables and accusing the libertarian of living in a harsh and brutal world. The libertarian supposes some kind of unknowable magic, brutally imposing its arbitrary will on people with zero consistency over time. You are like a boat on the high seas being tossed around by ever-changing winds and only minutes away from sinking beneath a large wave. There is nothing about „you“ making the decision; it just falls down from up high, produced by something, which can't be distinguished by any of us from a random number generator.

As you might have guessed by now, I don't bring up this judgement of libertarianism as merely a hypothetical. I really mean it; my world-view would gain nothing by my ability to choose a course of action without cause. This wouldn't be the ability to play god in a part of the universe, as the libertarian thinks. For me it would just seem like an unpredictable, uncontrollable randomness that alienates me from what I actually want to do in life. It also alienates me from the people around me, because I will never be able to know, even in principle, what motivates their decisions, which will always seem as arbitrary to me, as if I have never met them.

Of course, the libertarians will say that they do not view their doctrine through this harsh, brutalistic interpretation; and it is their right to do so. But my accusations that their world-view are pessimistic is just as valid as their accusations that our world-view is pessimistic. I also like to think that the preceding paragraphs have been rhetorically more persuasive than anything a libertarian might muster up against determinism; but I won't dwell on this argument for long, whose persuasiveness is entirely subjective.

Conclusion

Does all of this mean that there is no free will? No, it simply means that there is no free will which includes criteria 1) and 2). You could still have a theory which only incorporates 2), which would be some kind of compatibilism. You could also have a theory of free will that includes only 1), but that seems rather pointless to me, as in this case you can't meaningfully talk about your free will, but only some free will floating in the void that may belong to the universe at large, or to god, or maybe to nobody and nothing at all.

One important consequence is that if you are a compatibilist, then the determinateness of the will is actually a necessary condition for free will [2]. Only those choices of yours that are completely predictable in advance can even be candidates for a free choice, since anything not knowable beforehand is something that can't justifiably be connected to you. Even if only one decision in your entire life were unpredictable beforehand, you could still run all the arguments from above on that singular decision and conclude that it has nothing to do with you.

All that being said, I'd love to hear what a proponent of libertarianism might say to all this. After all, it doesn't seem true that all libertarians are too dumb to understand this. It also doesn't seem true that they want to be bossed around by arbitrary randomness. What am I missing that can help the libertarian out of this conundrum? Leave your comments on substack.

[1] I have been tempted to write „physical property“ instead of „knowable property“, since it sounds more concrete and is more familiar. However, nothing about this argument presupposes physicalism, or any other sort of metaphysics for that matter. Still for many it will be easier to read if you keep in mind that „knowable“ just means „physical“, if that is the metaphysics you support. For dualists it might even be worth while to read „physical or mental properties“ in place of „knowable property“. And likewise you can adapt this argument to any kind of metaphysics you hold.

[2] It is not necessary for strict determinism to be true. What I call momentary determinism is the thesis that the state of the universe at one point and the laws of nature uniquely determine your actions a few moments into the future, and this is necessary for free will. Eternal determinism is the stronger thesis that the state of the universe and the laws of nature uniquely determine arbitrarily far into the future and it is not necessary for free will. I can't rule out that on the quantum scale (or maybe some scale even smaller, which physics can't explain yet) there lies a fundamental element of indeterminism. And if this is the case it is very likely that this fundamental indeterminism can bring about a chain of events in a chaotic system, which eventually results in the indeterminism avalanching itself into macroscopic differences, which in turn influence your behaviour. For this reason I am totally agnostic to the proposition that we could have predicted all of your behaviour at the big bang, but I am very certain that this avalanching would take way longer than the handful of seconds in which most final decisions take place, since the stuff that makes you think (the neurons in your brain) is sufficiently large to behave nearly deterministically, even if the fundamental indeterminism is already at the level of quantum physics
